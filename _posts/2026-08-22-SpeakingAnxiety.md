I am still a beginner at public speaking, so I know how uncomfortable it can feel to stand in front of a group and suddenly forget what you wanted to say. A few days ago, I gave a three-minute speech at my Toastmasters club. Before my turn, I told myself, “I am here to learn and practice. I do not need to be perfect.” I felt quite calm while listening to other speakers.

Then my turn came.

Suddenly, my heart started beating faster, my hands began to shake, and my voice did not sound as steady as usual. The strange thing was that I did not feel very worried in my mind. My body simply reacted before I could control it.

After that experience, I started reading and watching videos about public speaking. I realized something important: feeling nervous is normal. We may not be able to completely remove nervousness, but we can learn how to speak even when we feel it.

Here are some simple tips that have helped me.

### 1. Do not try to be perfect

When I first started practicing, I thought good speakers should speak smoothly without mistakes. Now I think differently.

A speech is not an English test. Your goal is to communicate your idea, not to produce perfect sentences.

For example, if you forget a word, you can simply say:

"Let me say that in another way."

Then continue.

Making a small mistake does not mean your speech is bad. In fact, learning how to recover from mistakes is part of becoming a better speaker.

### 2. Prepare ideas, not every sentence

One mistake I often made was trying to memorize everything I wanted to say. This made me even more nervous because I was afraid of forgetting one sentence.

Now I usually prepare three main points instead.

For example, if I am talking about exercise, I might remember:

> Problem → Benefit → Example

Then I speak naturally around those three ideas.

This gives me a simple map to follow without forcing me to remember every word.

### 3. What should you do when your mind goes blank?

This is something I was very afraid of.

I used to think that if I forgot my words, my whole speech would fail. Now I know that a short pause is okay.

You can take a breath and say: *"Let me think for a moment."*

Or: *"The main point I want to make is ... "*

Then return to your main idea.

You do not need to remember the exact sentence. Just remember the idea.

For students, I think this is one of the most useful skills to practice: learning how to restart.

### 4. Use a simple structure for spontaneous questions

Job interviews and class discussions can be difficult because we cannot prepare every question.

I have found one simple structure very useful:

> Answer → Reason → Example

For example:

- Question: “Do you prefer working alone or in a team?”
- Answer: “I prefer working in a team.”
-> Reason: “I can learn from other people.”
-> Example: “For example, during my last project, my classmates helped me find a problem that I had missed.”

That is already a complete answer.

You do not need complicated vocabulary. A clear structure is often more important.

### 5. Remember that people are not watching you as closely as you think

When I am speaking, I sometimes feel that everyone is looking at every small mistake.

But when I listen to other speakers, I realize that I do not remember every mistake they make. I usually remember their main idea or an interesting example.

This is related to the **spotlight effect**: we often think other people notice us more than they actually do.

So, instead of thinking: *"Do I look nervous?"*

Try asking: *"Is my message clear?"*

That small change can help you focus on communication instead of worrying about yourself.

### 6. Practice a little, but practice often

I have learned that watching videos about public speaking is not enough. At some point, we have to actually speak.

For example, students can practice for just ten minutes:

- Pick a simple question.
- Think for 20 seconds.
- Speak out loud for one minute.
- Do it again with a different question.

You can also record yourself, practice with a friend, or join a speaking club.

The goal is not to become confident in one day. The goal is to become a little more comfortable each week.

### 7. After speaking, do not criticize everything

After my first speeches, I often remembered only the things I did badly.

Now I ask myself three simple questions:

- What went well?
- What was difficult?
- What will I practice next time?

For example: *“My introduction was clear. I spoke too quickly. Next time, I will pause after each main point.”*

This gives me something useful to practice instead of simply telling myself, “I was terrible.”

## Final Thoughts

I am still a beginner, and I still get nervous when I speak. But I have learned that confidence does not have to come before speaking. Sometimes, confidence grows because we keep speaking, making mistakes, and trying again.

My biggest lesson is simple: **Do not wait until you feel completely ready. Start small and keep practicing.**

You may still have a fast heartbeat. Your hands may still shake. You may sometimes forget a word.

That is okay.

The goal is not to become a perfect speaker. The goal is to become someone who can say, “I am nervous, but I can still speak.”
